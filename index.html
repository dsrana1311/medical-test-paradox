Scene 7 · Name it

What you've been doing has a name

Every run, you answered the same question: given that someone was flagged, how likely are they actually sick? You answered it by counting two piles. That counting is Bayes' theorem so don't take it from a textbook. Build it from your own town.

Drag each pile into its place in the fraction. Ask the question slowly: out of everyone who got flagged, how many are really sick?

And the second test? The same fraction, run twice:the 9% that came out of round one went in as the base rate for round two. "Updating your beliefs" has never meant anything more exotic than that chain.

The same arithmetic runs the real world: it's why a positive rare-disease screen is followed by a confirmatory test instead of a diagnosis and why spam filters that look superb still bury real mail.

Scene 9 · Epilogue

The theorem that sat in a drawer

Thomas Bayes never published it. An 18th-century minister with a taste for probability, he worked out the rule you've been using all lesson and filed it away. After his death in 1761, his friend Richard Price found the manuscript, understood what it was, and got it into print. Laplace later rediscovered it independently and gave it its modern form. The tool you just used to out-count a machine spent its first years as a dead man's note.

One last thing: the Bayesian trap

The same updating that kept you calm about one positive test can quietly work against you. Fail at something a few times and your inner probability of "I'm just bad at this" climbs, 9%, 50%, 91% until it feels like a law of nature and you stop trying. The math is doing its job; the experiment is broken. If you never act differently, you never generate the evidence that could move the number back down.

The escape is the move you made in Scene 6: don't treat one stream of evidence as final, go get different evidence. Order the second test. Change the approach. Your next belief is only as good as the experiments you're willing to run.

It reads your mail

Every message in your inbox is scored word by word, each word nudging the spam probability up or down. It's the same move as your piles: start from how rare a spam-word is, update on the evidence.

It never says "certain"

Bayes' rule can push a belief toward 0% or 100%, but never lands exactly on either, you watched the meter in Scene 6 crowd 100% without touching it. That's the philosophy in one image: knowledge as a number you keep updating, never a switch you flip.

That reflex is yours now

You counted your way to the truth, watched the paradox get worse as the disease got rarer, learned why the answer to a scary test is a second test, and called the stadium number before running any arithmetic. That's Bayes' theorem.
